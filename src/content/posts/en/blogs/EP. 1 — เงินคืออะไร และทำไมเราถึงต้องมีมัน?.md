## Why isn't being good at your job enough?

Have you ever wondered why everyone tells us to invest? Whether it's CK, Choke, or other investment channels, the advice seems to be the same. Why can't we just work hard, get good at what we do, and save our money for the future?

Most people would probably answer: inflation. If we leave our money sitting there, the same amount may buy less later. But have you ever asked why money has to lose value in the first place? Why should the money we worked for be worth less? And when people say deflation is bad, is it really?

We use money almost every day, but we might never have stopped to understand how it works. I think it's time to start with that question: what is money?

Over thousands of years, people have used shells, metals, gold, banknotes, and numbers in bank accounts as money. How did we move between them? Why did we stop using gold and start using paper that we can't even exchange back for gold?

I'd like to start with a simpler question. Why did people need money at all?

## Why do people need money?

People have exchanged goods and services for a long time. A rice farmer might exchange rice for tools, or for food they don't grow themselves. This direct exchange of goods is called barter.

> **Note:** Barter was one form of exchange in the past, but there is no evidence that every society started with barter before using money. Debt and accounting systems also existed in ancient societies.

It sounds simple enough. The problem is that the person who has what you want may not want what you have. The farmer might need tools, but the toolmaker may already have plenty of rice and want fish instead. The farmer then needs to find someone who has fish and wants rice, before taking those fish back to the toolmaker.

Economists call this the **double coincidence of wants**. For an exchange to happen, both people need to want what the other person has. Finding that match becomes harder as the number of people and goods grows.

It becomes much easier if there is something many people agree to accept. This is one of money's main functions: acting as a **medium of exchange**.

People haven't always used the same thing as money. Different places have used shells, salt, beads, silver, and gold. So why did some things become widely accepted, while others didn't?

## What makes something useful as money?

Cowrie shells are one example. They were used as money in parts of Asia and Africa. They were small, easy to carry, and easy to count. But they had a problem: they were scarce in some places and plentiful in others.

When more shells arrived, their value could fall. That brings us to one useful property of money: its supply shouldn't be too easy to increase. But why might a larger supply reduce its value?

Imagine an economy with 100 units of money and a certain quantity of goods and services. Someone holding 10 units has 10% of all the money. Now suppose the money supply grows to 1,000 units, while the quantity of goods and services stays the same and that person still has only 10 units.

Their share has fallen from 10% to 1%. That doesn't mean they can immediately buy ten times less. Prices don't always move in direct proportion to the money supply. But if there is more money without more goods and services, each unit may lose purchasing power.

That's one reason changes in the money supply can affect its value. Scarcity alone isn't enough, though. Money should also last, resist damage, be easy to carry, divide into smaller units, and be possible to check for authenticity.

Above all, people have to accept it. Something can be extremely rare and still be useless for exchange if nobody wants it.

Gold has many of these properties. It has been used as money and as a way to store wealth in many civilizations. It lasts, doesn't easily corrode, and can be melted down and divided into different quantities. Getting more gold also takes labor, time, and resources. We can't produce more instantly just because we want it.

Gold doesn't have a fixed value, of course. Its price also changes with supply and demand. But these properties helped it become widely accepted.

### When gold becomes inconvenient

Gold isn't convenient for every transaction, particularly as trade grows. Moving large quantities costs money, adds weight, and creates a risk of theft. Checking that it is genuine every time is difficult too.

People developed ways to trade and settle debts without moving gold around for every payment. One idea that became important was the IOU: "I owe you."

## IOUs: when money becomes a promise

Keeping track of debts isn't new. People have recorded who owes whom, and what they must repay, for thousands of years. Some societies recorded debts and trade on clay tablets.

An IOU is a record or promise that someone must pay something to someone else. As trade developed, documents representing debts and claims to payment became more important.

One example is a receipt for deposited gold. A person who left gold with a trusted custodian would receive a document confirming their right to get it back. If other people trusted that document too, it could sometimes be passed on as payment.

Instead of withdrawing gold every time, people could transfer the document that represented a claim to it. The paper itself wasn't what made it valuable. It was the belief that its issuer could actually return the gold.

This shows that payment doesn't always require handing over a valuable object directly. Sometimes what changes hands is a claim or a promise. But that raises another question: what happens if the person making the promise can't keep it?

## Banks, lending, and trust

As trade expanded, people needed places to keep their money and sources of funding for their businesses. Banks developed beyond storing money or gold for depositors. They could also lend some funds and earn interest.

People with spare money could deposit it, while people who needed capital could borrow it. A merchant might borrow money to buy stock and repay the loan after selling it. The bank would earn interest. But there was a problem.

Deposited money wasn't all held as cash at every moment. Some funds were lent out or held in other assets, while some depositors could ask for their money back immediately. A bank might therefore lack enough cash to repay every depositor at once.

The system could keep running as long as people didn't all withdraw at the same time. Trouble began when trust disappeared. If depositors feared the bank couldn't repay them, each had a reason to withdraw before everyone else. The more people withdrew, the harder it became for the bank to find cash.

A bank might have to sell assets quickly or try to recover some loans. That could cause problems for businesses and borrowers too. Depositors' worries could turn into a banking crisis. One example happened in the United States in 1907.

## The crisis of 1907 and the beginnings of the Fed

In 1907, the United States faced a major financial crisis. Many financial institutions didn't keep all deposits as cash. They lent funds or invested in other assets. Some held relatively little cash compared with deposits their customers could withdraw.

### The run on Knickerbocker Trust

After a stock speculation failed, reports spread about connections between some financial institution executives and the speculators. People worried that their deposits weren't safe and began withdrawing money.

One institution hit hard was Knickerbocker Trust Company in New York. On October 22, 1907, depositors rushed to withdraw their money. Almost eight million dollars was withdrawn within a few hours, before Knickerbocker Trust suspended operations because it couldn't obtain cash quickly enough.

This rush to withdraw is called a bank run. The problem didn't stop with one institution. Fear spread to others. More people wanted their money back, while financial institutions scrambled to find cash.

The crisis became severe enough that J.P. Morgan helped coordinate banks and financiers to put funds together and support the financial system. But it raised a question: if a loss of trust could shake the country's financial system, who should step in during a crisis?

### The creation of the Fed

The United States began reforming its financial system after the crisis. In 1913, it established the Federal Reserve, usually called the Fed, as its central bank.

One of its important functions is supporting financial stability. It can provide emergency loans to banks experiencing temporary cash shortages, helping reduce the risk of trouble spreading through the system.

That creates another question. If a central bank can add money to the system to help banks, who decides when to do it, and how much to add?

Adding money may ease some crises, but it can also affect money's value and how people behave. Economists disagree about this. Some believe central banks can make crises less severe. Others worry that too much support encourages banks to take greater risks because they expect help when things go wrong.

These disagreements became more visible during the twentieth century, particularly as the world faced wars and major economic crises.

## Where does today's money come from?

We've looked at exchanging goods, using shells and gold, transferring claims to gold, and developing banks and institutions to support the financial system during crises. Making payments more convenient has often brought new problems to solve. One recurring problem is trust.

Whether money is gold, paper, or a bank balance, it works as money because people are willing to accept it and pass it on. But the story doesn't end with the creation of the Fed.

World War I began in 1914, followed by the major economic crisis of 1929 and World War II in 1939. These events helped change the world's monetary system. In 1971, the United States ended dollar-to-gold convertibility for foreign governments and central banks under the international monetary arrangements of that time. That was an important step toward the system we use today.

How did it happen? Why do governments and central banks play such a large role in the money we use? And how does that connect to the questions at the start: why does money lose purchasing power, and why do we keep being told to invest?
